# Project Intention Document: Mental Health Trends Analysis

## Background and Objectives  
Mental health – the emotional, psychological, and social well-being of individuals – profoundly influences how people think, feel, and act.  Globally, mental disorders have soared in prevalence; WHO estimates nearly **970 million people** lived with a mental disorder in 2019 (anxiety and depression being most common).  In 2025, WHO reported “more than 1 billion people” are currently affected, imposing immense human and economic costs.  Mental illness is already the leading cause of years lived with disability worldwide.  In the U.S., mental health conditions are rising rapidly: for example, CDC data show that the prevalence of depression among adolescents and adults **increased by 60% over the past decade**.  The COVID-19 pandemic further exacerbated the crisis – the first pandemic year saw a *25% global increase* in anxiety and depression prevalence.  These trends have been especially acute among youth and women, with U.S. high-schoolers reporting persistent sadness rising from 28% in 2011 to 40% in 2023, and 20% having seriously considered suicide in 2023.  

The *Mental Health Trends Analysis* project aims to **understand and quantify these emerging patterns** before any technical development. Key objectives are to assess the evolving scope of mental health problems, identify affected populations and risk factors, survey available data and metrics, and outline how analytics can yield actionable insights.  Ultimately, this will inform policymakers, providers, and communities in planning interventions, allocating resources, and monitoring progress.

## Literature Survey: Trends and Epidemiology  
Recent academic and policy literature paints a stark picture of worsening mental health.  Globally, studies confirm mental disorders are the leading non-fatal disease burden and are increasing, especially in children and adolescents.  A 2025 global analysis found the mental disorder burden in youth rose sharply after 2019, driven largely by population growth and a surge in prevalence rates.  Disparities are evident: high-income countries show higher absolute prevalence, but **over 80%** of people with mental disorders live in low- and middle-income countries, where severe workforce shortages and stigma impede care.  WHO warns that despite some policy progress, most countries underinvest in mental health – median government spending remains only **2% of total health budgets** – and suicide mortality declines are insufficient to meet global targets.

In the U.S. and other high-income settings, detailed surveillance reveals similar patterns.  Multiple analyses report rising anxiety and depression, especially among youth.  For example, CDC found that nearly **4 in 10 U.S. teens** felt persistently sad or hopeless in 2023, and 1 in 5 adults have been diagnosed with a depressive disorder.  New CDC reports (Apr 2025) show that nearly 40% of people aged 12+ with depression received therapy in the past year, and over 10% of U.S. adults took antidepressant medication in 2023 (women twice as likely as men).  Behavioral health researchers emphasize that these trends reflect a broader “behavioral health crisis”: the prevalence of mental health conditions and substance use disorders is rising while access to care is limited by provider shortages and insurance gaps. 

Key findings from surveys and reports (citing CDC, WHO, and academic sources) include:  
- **Increasing prevalence** –  WHO: 970M global cases (2019), rising to 1B+ (2025); CDC: U.S. depression up 60% in past decade; youth sadness up from 28% (2011) to 40% (2023).  
- **Population disparities** – Women and adolescents show higher rates; LGBTQ and some racial/ethnic groups face even greater burdens.  WHO notes women are disproportionately impacted by anxiety/depression, and suicide disproportionately affects young people (727,000 deaths in 2021 globally).  
- **Comorbidity and Costs** – Mental illness often co-occurs with physical illness.  For example, people with mental disorders have higher rates of chronic diseases and incur ~75% higher health care costs.  Depression and anxiety alone cost the global economy ~$1 trillion annually.  
- **Service Utilization** – Although need is rising, service use remains inadequate.  Only about half of U.S. youth or young adults with mental health conditions receive treatment.  In 2022 there were ~5.9 million U.S. ED visits primarily for mental disorders.  National data channels report ~12% of U.S. adults with frequent anxiety and ~4.8% with regular depression, highlighting a substantial treatment gap.  

These sources collectively underscore a **growing unmet need**: many affected people remain untreated, leading to long-term consequences in education, employment, and health.  The academic literature repeatedly calls for better surveillance and integrated datasets to guide targeted interventions.

## Data Sources and Surveillance Systems  
A wide range of public data sources and reports track mental health status, behaviors, and outcomes at the population level.  Key sources include:

- **National surveys (U.S.)**:  *NHANES* and *NHIS* provide annual estimates of anxiety and depression prevalence and care use. *NSDUH* (SAMHSA) estimates mental illness and substance use disorder prevalence at national/state levels. *BRFSS* adds state-by-state risk factor data, and the *Youth Risk Behavior Surveillance System* (YRBSS) monitors adolescent mental health behaviors (e.g. sadness, suicide attempts) over time. *NSCH* surveys children’s health and well-being, including mental health and access to services.  
- **Surveillance reports**:  CDC’s *Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Report* and NCHS press releases regularly publish mental health findings (e.g. trends in depression, suicide, treatment).  The *CDC Mental Health Data Channel* aggregates recent data from its surveys and emergency department data to highlight national trends. SAMHSA’s *National Survey on Drug Use and Health* annual reports include mental illness estimates for youth and adults.  
- **Healthcare data**:  Health claims and utilization data (e.g. Medicare/Medicaid claims, MarketScan) can reveal service usage and costs for mental health diagnoses. Hospital databases (HCUP) and CDC’s *National Hospital Ambulatory Medical Care Survey* track mental-health-related visits.  Vital statistics record suicide mortality.  Note that many healthcare data sources do not fully capture outpatient psychotherapy or community services.  
- **International data**:  WHO’s *Global Health Estimates*, *Mental Health Atlas*, and the *Global Burden of Disease (GBD)* study (IHME) provide cross-country comparisons and long-term trends of mental disorder prevalence and DALYs. OECD and World Bank repositories hold data on health financing and workforce.  
- **Specialty data repositories**:  Data.gov and NIH/CDC portals host datasets (e.g. Mental Health Study Supplements, Epidemiologic Follow-up Studies).  Research consortia (e.g. ABCD neuroimaging, NIMH data archives) offer open datasets for longitudinal studies.  The JMIR directory notes major surveys and repositories (Data.CDC.gov, ICPSR, etc.) that aggregate mental health data.  

Each source has strengths (large sample, national representativeness) and limitations (cross-sectional snapshots, self-report bias).  For example, [3] notes that surveys like NHIS or YRBSS are cross-sectional, which “provides nationally representative snapshots” but limit **longitudinal trajectory analysis**.  In contrast, studies like NHANES, NSDUH, and ABCD offer repeated measures or rich detail but may have smaller samples or restrictive access.  We will leverage a mix of these sources to capture prevalence, service use, and outcomes across populations and time.

## Key Stakeholders and Care Pathways  
The mental health system involves many stakeholders:  

- **Government & Public Health** – Federal and state agencies (HHS, CDC, SAMHSA, NIH/NIMH, state health departments) set policies, fund programs, and maintain surveillance.  They rely on trend analyses to guide initiatives and resource allocation.   
- **Healthcare Providers** – This includes *primary care clinicians* (often the first contact for mental health), *specialists* (psychiatrists, psychologists, therapists), as well as *emergency and inpatient services*.  There is a growing emphasis on **integrated care**: co-located behavioral health in primary care settings to improve access.  Providers need data to identify patient needs, monitor outcomes, and optimize care pathways.  
- **Payers** – Public (Medicaid/Medicare) and private insurers cover mental health services.  Notably, Medicaid is the *largest payer for mental health care in the U.S.*, with half of Medicaid enrollees having a mental health diagnosis.  Payers track utilization and costs; parity laws and value-based models drive interest in preventive strategies and cost-effective care.  
- **Patients and Families** – Individuals with mental health conditions and their support networks have lived experience insights.  They advocate for better services and reduced stigma.  Community organizations (e.g. NAMI, Mental Health America) also act as intermediaries, providing resources and raising awareness.  
- **Employers and Educators** – Workplaces and schools are increasingly recognized as critical settings. Employers (through EAPs or health benefits) and educators (through school counselors) play roles in early identification and support.  Data on work productivity losses and student well-being are relevant to these stakeholders.  
- **Justice and Social Systems** – Legal and social services intersect with mental health (e.g. criminal justice, child welfare).  Trends analysis can inform diversion programs and community support initiatives.  

**Care pathways** in mental health are often “stepped”: mild cases may be managed in primary care with screening, brief counseling or digital tools; moderate cases are referred to outpatient psychotherapy or medication management; severe cases require intensive specialty care or hospitalization.  Throughout, care coordination and follow-up are crucial.  For example, integrating mental health providers into primary care teams can reduce treatment delays.  Telehealth and mobile crisis teams have expanded access, especially after COVID-19.  **Suicide prevention** and crisis response (e.g. 988 lifeline) are critical components of the system.  

## System-level Challenges and Barriers  
Analysis of mental health trends must account for systemic challenges:  

- **Resource Shortages** – There is a chronic shortage of mental health professionals.  Even in high-income countries, wait times are long; in low-income regions the shortage is *severe*, with WHO noting many countries have fewer than one mental health worker per 100,000 people.  Investment is low – on average governments still spend only ~2% of health budgets on mental health.  
- **Access and Equity** – Stigma, cost, and insurance barriers prevent many from seeking care.  Nearly 70% of people with serious mental health issues either do not seek help or rely solely on primary care.  Disparities by race, income, and geography are large. For example, minority and rural populations face greater obstacles and lower treatment rates.  Social determinants (poverty, housing instability, violence) both contribute to mental health issues and limit access to care.  
- **Fragmented Data and Services** – Mental health care is often siloed from physical health care.  Data are fragmented across sectors (health, education, criminal justice).  Electronic health records contain valuable information but are difficult to share due to privacy (HIPAA) and system interoperability issues.  As a result, there is no single repository of comprehensive mental health outcomes, making trend analysis complex.  
- **Measurement Limitations** – Surveys rely on self-reported symptoms or screening items rather than clinical diagnoses, introducing measurement error.  Cultural differences in reporting and varying diagnostic criteria limit comparability across groups.  Many data sources are cross-sectional, making it hard to infer causality or individual patient pathways.  Data lags also mean real-time changes (e.g. sudden crises) can be missed.  
- **External Stressors** – Population-level events (pandemics, economic downturns, social unrest) can cause abrupt mental health changes.  The 2020 COVID-19 pandemic is a prime example: WHO reported major service disruptions and risk factors (isolation, bereavement) that spiked mental illness.  Analytical plans must consider such confounders and adjust for them.

Recognizing these barriers is crucial for realistic project planning.  For instance, undercounting of affected individuals suggests we may need to use proxy indicators (like suicide rates or hospitalizations) to infer trends.  Privacy restrictions mean we will rely heavily on aggregated/public data rather than patient-level EHR.

## Analytical Opportunities and Key Metrics  
Despite the challenges, there are rich opportunities to apply data analytics:

- **Trend and Pattern Analysis** – Using longitudinal survey and claims data, we can quantify changes in prevalence, incidence, and service use over time.  For example, time-series models could evaluate whether the upward trend in adolescent depression continues post-2023.  Geographic analyses (state or county levels) can identify hotspots of unmet need.  
- **Predictive Modeling** – Machine learning models could predict individuals or communities at high risk of mental health crises or suicide, enabling targeted interventions.  Existing work uses AI on social media and wearable data to detect early warning signs.  Hospitals could use predictive analytics on EHR data (where available) to flag patients needing extra support.  
- **Program Evaluation** – Analytics can assess the impact of policies (e.g. mental health parity laws, expansion of Medicaid coverage) or programs (school-based mental health services, teletherapy initiatives).  Difference-in-differences or synthetic control methods might measure outcome changes pre/post implementation.  
- **Resource Allocation** – Data-driven optimization can help allocate scarce provider resources.  For example, demand forecasts from demographic projections could inform where to build new clinics or recruit providers.  
- **Integrated Data Insights** – Combining datasets (claims, surveys, social media) may reveal associations between mental health and factors like unemployment, housing instability, or community violence.  Multivariate analyses can uncover key social determinants to target.  

**Key metrics** to track include:  
- **Prevalence and Incidence** – e.g. percentage of population with diagnosed depression or anxiety (from surveys/claims), new case rates.  
- **Service Utilization** – numbers and rates of outpatient visits for mental health, hospital admissions for psychiatric conditions, emergency department visits with mental health diagnoses.  
- **Treatment Engagement** – therapy or counseling rates, medication prescription rates (e.g. % of depressed patients on antidepressants).  Example: CDC found >10% of U.S. adults took antidepressants in 2023.  
- **Outcome Measures** – suicide rates (suicide per 100,000; 48,824 deaths in 2024), self-harm hospitalization rates, patient-reported outcomes if available (e.g. PHQ-9 scores).  
- **Equity Indicators** – disparities in the above metrics by race/ethnicity, income, region, and age.  Behavioral health trackers often stratify by demographic group.  
- **Process Indicators** – screening rates in primary care, wait times for mental health appointments, coverage rates (insurance reimbursement for mental health).  
- **Economic Indicators** – direct costs (Medicaid/Medicare spending on mental health) and indirect costs (lost productivity, unemployment among those with mental illness).  

Each metric will need to be contextualized.  For instance, a rise in diagnosed prevalence could reflect either more illness or better detection/screening.  Mixed-methods (qualitative review of policy changes) may help interpret quantitative trends. 

## Data Limitations and Considerations  
When analyzing the data, we will carefully note limitations:

- **Survey Bias** – Self-report instruments (e.g. NHIS mental health questions) may undercount due to stigma or misunderstanding.  Some groups may be less likely to participate in surveys.  Comparisons over time must account for any changes in survey wording or methodology.  
- **Cross-Sectional Data** – Many key datasets (YRBSS, NSDUH, NHIS) are cross-sectional.  They tell us *what* proportions are affected, but not *who* remains ill or recovers over time.  We cannot infer individual trajectories unless using longitudinal datasets (e.g. MEPS, ABCD).  
- **Clinical vs. Non-Clinical Diagnoses** – Surveys often use screening questions (e.g. “feeling down, depressed, hopeless”), which indicate symptoms but not confirmed clinical diagnoses.  Conversely, claims data include only those who sought care.  We must be cautious equating survey prevalence with diagnosed disorder prevalence.  
- **Data Access and Granularity** – Some ideal data (detailed claims or EHR) may be inaccessible due to privacy.  We may rely on aggregate public-use files.  Geographic detail may be limited (national/state vs. local).  
- **Changing Definitions and Practices** – Diagnostic criteria (e.g. DSM changes), health-seeking behavior, and coding practices can change over time, affecting trend continuity.  For example, increased awareness of mental health might raise diagnosis rates independent of true prevalence changes.  
- **Confounding Factors** – External events (e.g. pandemic, economic cycles) can abruptly alter mental health patterns.  We will consider exogenous variables and possibly include them as covariates or separate trend analyses.  

Despite these issues, leveraging multiple data sources can mitigate individual weaknesses.  For example, survey and administrative data can be triangulated to validate trends.  Rigorous documentation of each data source’s scope and bias will ensure transparent interpretation of results.

## Conclusion and Next Steps  
In summary, mental health conditions are a growing public health priority with broad societal impacts.  Our research finds that **mental illness prevalence and service needs are rising** across the population, particularly among youth, with persistent gaps in access and coverage.  Stakeholders from government to healthcare and communities are invested in understanding these trends.  Public datasets (CDC/NIMH surveys, WHO reports, etc.) offer rich information, though challenges like data fragmentation and stigma-induced underreporting must be managed.  

The *Mental Health Trends Analysis* project will synthesize this evidence into a clear picture of the current landscape and future projections.  By quantifying key metrics (prevalence, treatment, outcomes) and identifying high-risk groups, the analysis can guide data-driven decisions – such as where to expand mental health services, what outcomes to measure, and how to evaluate interventions.  Importantly, the project will emphasize equity and cross-disciplinary collaboration, as recommended in the literature, to ensure findings inform holistic, population-level solutions.  

**Next steps** include: finalizing data access (surveys, administrative records), formulating specific analytic questions (e.g. “How have depression rates changed by age group since 2010?”), and developing a timeline for reporting.  The insights gained will lay the groundwork for subsequent stages (e.g. dashboard development), but for now they will help shape a robust, targeted analytics approach.
